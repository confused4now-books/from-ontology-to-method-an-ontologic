Abstract
The quantitative-qualitative divide in social research reflects a deeper philosophical problem: the epistemic fallacy, which collapses questions about what exists into questions about how we can know it. When ontological commitments remain implicit, competing methodological approaches rest on incompatible assumptions that generate irresolvable debate. This paper does two things. First, it diagnoses the epistemic fallacy as the root cause of the methodological divide. Second, it demonstrates a systematic response through a hybrid framework integrating Bhaskar's dialectical critical realism, Archer's morphogenetic approach, and Knio's immanent causality morphogenetic approach. The result is a demonstration that principled methodological pluralism requires prior ontological work.
Keywords
Dialectical Critical Realism, Morphogenetic Approach, Research Methods, Social Ontology, Interdisciplinarity
Introduction
The social sciences remain divided between quantitative and qualitative approaches. This division is typically framed as epistemological: quantitative approaches emphasize objectivity and the subject-object distinction, seeking knowledge independent of the researcher. On the other hand qualitative approaches typically engage with subjectivity and the subject-subject relation that should foster understanding through interpretive engagement. However, treating this as an epistemological question, about how we can know, already obscures a more fundamental problem: what exists in (social) reality that we are trying to know? The subject-object distinction addresses our epistemic access to reality, but it cannot determine what that reality consists of. Both objective measurement and subjective interpretation require prior specification of what exists that can be measured or interpreted: do social structures possess causal powers independent of individual consciousness? Do meanings exist only through actors' interpretations? These are ontological questions that epistemological frameworks cannot resolve. This division has profound consequences for addressing complex social problems requiring genuine interdisciplinary collaboration. When researchers from different traditions approach shared problems such as climate change adaptation, inequality reduction, or public health crises, they tend to talk past one another, not because they employ different procedures, but because they make incompatible assumptions about what social reality consists of and how its different aspects relate to one another. Moreover, these assumptions remain largely implicit, thus preventing the cumulative knowledge building and methodological coordination that complex problems would require.
Extensive efforts to bridge this divide have produced sophisticated mixed methods designs (Creswell and Clark 2018; Tashakkori and Teddlie 2010), pragmatic approaches emphasizing what works regardless of paradigmatic origin (Morgan 2007; Rorty 1982), as well as theoretical syntheses attempting to transcend dualisms through concepts like structuration (Giddens 1984) or practice (Bourdieu 1977). Despite these considerable achievements, the fundamental division persists because these solutions address symptoms rather than causes.
This paper argues that transcending the quantitative-qualitative divide needs explicit ontological foundations rather than methodological synthesis or pragmatic eclecticism. We develop this argument through an integration of Roy Bhaskar's dialectical critical realism (Bhaskar 1993), Margaret Archer's morphogenetic approach (Archer 1995), and Karim Knio's immanent causality morphogenetic approach (2018; 2020). This integration aims to provide a systematic framework for understanding how ontological commitments about the nature of social reality should inform methodological choices, particularly regarding the relationship between social structures and human agency.
The paper will proceed in three parts. First, we examine how what Bhaskar (1975; 1979) calls the epistemic fallacy (the reduction of ontological questions about what exists to epistemological questions about what we can know) obscures recognition that the quantitative-qualitative divide constitutes an unnecessary obstacle to realist research, establishing the necessity for explicit social ontology. Second, we outline our hybrid ontological framework, explaining how our hybrid model of Bhaskar's dialectical approach enriched by Archer's and Knio's insights provides tools for explicit ontological analysis. Third, we work through each moment of Bhaskar's four-moment dialectic (1M through 4D), demonstrating how ontological commitments at each level inform methodological approaches to researching structures and agency. By systematically linking ontological commitments to methodological requirements across these four moments, we demonstrate both why explicit social ontology proves necessary and how it enables principled methodological pluralism grounded in understanding what different aspects of social reality actually are and how they operate.
The Epistemic Fallacy and the Necessity for Explicit Social Ontology
Diagnosing the Problem: Why Methodological Solutions Prove Insufficient
Methodological integration efforts have not resolved the quantitative-qualitative divide because the division reflects ontological problems rather than merely technical disagreements. Understanding this requires examining what Bhaskar (1975; 1979) identifies as the epistemic fallacy: the reduction of ontological questions about what exists to epistemological questions about what we can know. This fallacy takes various forms across different philosophical traditions. Empiricism defines existence through observability, treating what can be measured or detected as constituting the real (Hume 1748), manifesting in contemporary debates through logical positivism's verification principle (Ayer 1936; Carnap 1928), Comtean positivism's restriction of science to observable facts and their laws (Comte 1853). Idealism treats reality as constituted through thought, whether individual consciousness or collective discourse (Berkeley 1710), manifesting in contemporary debates through Weber's (1922) verstehen and emphasis on subjective meaning, Schutz's (1972) phenomenological approach treating social reality as constituted through actors' lived experience, strong social constructionism arguing that social entities have no existence apart from the discursive practices through which they are continually enacted, such that to ask what a social entity 'really' is independently of those practices is to ask an empty question (Berger and Luckmann 1966), and poststructuralist claims that there is nothing outside the text (Derrida 1967). Despite apparent opposition, both positions commit the same fundamental error: collapsing ontological questions into epistemological ones by defining what exists through how we know it.
The epistemic fallacy obscures both the problem and its solution. By conflating what exists with what we can know, it generates the appearance that methodological divisions reflect fundamental features of reality rather than consequences of unexamined philosophical assumptions. If existence is defined through observability, quantitative measurement appears naturally suited because it provides systematic procedures for detecting observable patterns, such as counting the frequency of a behaviour across a population and treating the resulting rate as the social fact to be explained. Unobservable entities like social structures or causal mechanisms become either eliminated as metaphysical speculation or reduced to observable regularities (Hempel 1965). If social reality is defined as constituted through meaning and interpretation, qualitative methods appear naturally suited for accessing meanings through ethnography, interviews, and textual analysis (Geertz 1973; Winch 1958). Contemporary debates continue this pattern: arguments for mixed methods integration often presume paradigmatic incommensurability while seeking technical solutions (Tshakkori and Teddlie 2003), critical approaches challenge positivist assumptions without examining their own ontological commitments (Guba and Lincoln 1994), and calls for methodological pluralism focus on technique diversity rather than ontological foundations (Denzin and Lincoln 2005; Maxwell 2013). Both historical and contemporary routes generate the same problem: defining what exists through epistemological access, then treating resulting methodological approaches as reflecting natural divisions in reality itself.
This collapse generates three interconnected problems. First, it makes scientific progress mysterious and inexplicable. If theories structure experience in incommensurable ways, we cannot explain why some theories prove more successful except through appeals to power dynamics or rhetoric (Kuhn 2012). Paradigm shifts appear as conversion experiences rather than rational choices based on explanatory adequacy. Second, it prevents error identification. If existence is defined epistemologically, we cannot discover we were wrong about what exists, only that we changed conceptual frameworks (Bhaskar 1979; Sayer 2008). The possibility of being mistaken about reality requires distinguishing what exists from what we know about it. Third, it undermines realism itself. If we cannot distinguish what exists independently from what we know, scientific investigation appears to study our own conceptual constructions rather than an independent reality (Collier 1994; Norrie 2010).
For social research, the epistemic fallacy proves particularly problematic because it encourages treating social reality as inherently epistemological, existing only through being known or constructed. This prevents recognizing that social structures, institutions, and cultural systems exist and operate whether or not we have adequate theories about them, and whether or not actors themselves comprehend the structural mechanisms shaping their possibilities (Archer 1995; Elder-Vass 2012). Avoiding the epistemic fallacy requires maintaining distinction between the intransitive dimension (what exists independently) and the transitive dimension (our necessarily fallible knowledge), enabling us to be wrong about what exists precisely because existence and knowledge constitute distinct questions. Transcending the quantitative-qualitative divide requires reversing the relationship between ontology and methodology: beginning from explicit commitments about what exists independently, then deriving methodological requirements from these ontological specifications.
Establishing the Necessity for Explicit Social Ontology
Three arguments establish that adequately addressing the quantitative-qualitative divide requires making ontological commitments explicit rather than pursuing further methodological integration or paradigmatic synthesis. First, methodological choices necessarily embody ontological assumptions whether acknowledged or not (Danermark 2001; Sayer 2008). Choosing to measure assumes relevant phenomena can be captured through measurement; choosing to interpret assumes meanings constitute essential aspects; choosing experimental manipulation assumes causal relationships can be isolated; choosing ethnographic immersion assumes understanding requires grasping practices from participants' perspectives. These reflect assumptions about what exists, what matters, and how different aspects relate (Blaikie 1993; Gorski 2017). Making these explicit enables examining their adequacy rather than allowing them to operate invisibly, shaping research directions through implicit influence.
Second, avoiding the epistemic fallacy by maintaining distinction between ontology and epistemology reveals that apparent dichotomies between observation and interpretation, measurement and meaning, objectivity and subjectivity reflect epistemological confusions rather than ontological necessities (Bhaskar 1975; Bhaskar 1979). This can be overcome by defining what exists independently and then determining which methods can adequately access those aspects (Collier 1994; Lawson 1997). This recognition does not eliminate disagreement but shifts debate to more productive ground. Researchers can legitimately disagree about what actually exists in social reality, whether structures possess emergent properties irreducible to individuals, whether meanings exist independently of interpretation, whether causal mechanisms operate beneath observable patterns. Our claim in this paper is not to settle these positions, even where we illustrate one, but to show that taking the epistemic fallacy seriously requires the kind of explicit ontological specification this paper undertakes. These disagreements cannot be resolved by appealing to a God's-eye view of reality independent of all conceptual schemes, a position critiqued since Kant's demonstration that we cannot access things-in-themselves apart from our conceptual frameworks. Acknowledging that we cannot achieve direct unmediated access to reality, however, does not mean we should abandon attempts to specify what exists independently of our knowledge of it.
Critical realism addresses this through the concept of the ontological referent: social objects exist independently of our theories about them, and making explicit ontological commitments about what these objects are, however fallible those commitments may prove, they provide a guide for conducting research rather than being final claim to certainty. This independence of the referent from our theorising about it is what allows competing ontological specifications to be assessed systematically against one another, rather than treated as equally valid descriptions of a reality with no determinate character of its own (Bhaskar 1975; Bhaskar 1979). While we cannot step outside our conceptual schemes to compare them directly with reality, we can make explicit ontological claims about what exists and how it operates, then test these claims against reality through theory development and empirical investigation. Different ontological specifications generate different predictions about what we should observe under varying conditions, enabling comparative assessment of explanatory adequacy. The ontological referent thus enables productive debate about competing specifications rather than deferring to paradigmatic conventions or methodological allegiances. When researchers would make explicit ontological claims, for example, that social structures possess emergent properties operating independently of individual consciousness, or that meanings exist only through interpretive practices these claims could be systematically evaluated through their capacity to explain phenomena. Currently, however, researchers rarely make such claims explicit; instead, ontological assumptions remain embedded within methodological choices and paradigmatic allegiances, preventing systematic evaluation of their adequacy. The ontological referent moves methodological debates from irresolvable epistemological standoffs, where competing paradigms remain incommensurable because they define existence through different modes of access, to empirically and theoretically adjudicable disagreements about what actually exists and how it operates in social reality.
Third, beginning from explicit ontological commitments makes methodological pluralism possible and necessary in ways that current approaches cannot achieve. The crucial question becomes: how do researchers make methodological choices? Currently, choices typically derive from epistemological commitments (what counts as valid knowledge) or paradigmatic allegiances (identification with particular research traditions), not from systematic analysis of what exists and requires investigation. Researchers trained in quantitative traditions default to measurement and statistical analysis; those trained in qualitative traditions default to interpretation and thick description; those committed to critical approaches prioritize emancipatory goals over ontological specification. These choices may prove appropriate for particular investigations, but their appropriateness remains unexamined because the basis for choice remains implicit. When ontology precedes methodology, we can recognize that social reality possesses multiple aspects with different ontological characteristics, each requiring investigation through methods adequate to those specific characteristics. Structures operating at the level of emergent properties, for instance, require methods capable of identifying collective-level patterns that cannot be reduced to individual behaviours. Similarly, meanings operating through interpretive practices require methods capable of accessing how actors understand their situations, while causal mechanisms operating through powers and liabilities require methods capable of tracing how mechanisms generate outcomes through their interaction in specific contexts. Ontology-first approaches enable principled methodological pluralism because they specify what we assume exists, then adapt data collection and analysis to the specific contexts and conditions under which these ontological entities operate. This shifts the basis for methodological choice from epistemological or paradigmatic grounds to ontological grounds: methods are selected not because they align with particular theories of knowledge or research traditions, but because they can adequately access specific aspects of social reality given what those aspects are and how they operate. This ontologically-grounded pluralism proves essential for investigating stratified social reality, where adequate explanation requires accessing multiple levels that cannot all be captured through any single methodological approach.
The Hybrid Ontological Framework and Paper Structure
While Dialectical Critical Realism (DCR) addresses multiple ontological debates, this paper focuses on how our hybrid model of Bhaskar's four-moment dialectic (1M through 4D), enriched by Archer's temporal analysis of structural conditioning and social interaction and Knio's Immanent Causality Morphogenetic Approach (ICMA), provides a framework for reconceptualizing the relationship between social structures and human agency. These three frameworks exhibit some theoretical tensions regarding the precise nature of emergence, the degree of systemic persistence, and the relationship between transcendent and immanent causality. However, they share fundamental commitments about social reality as stratified (operating through multiple irreducible levels), emergent (with collective properties possessing causal powers not reducible to individual properties), and temporally dynamic (involving both structural conditioning and agential transformation). These tensions are themselves worth debate and discussion: they represent genuine philosophical questions about the nature of social reality rather than mere terminological differences. Having an ontological referent as the starting point provides the basis for productive debate about these tensions, enabling researchers to assess which ontological specifications offer better explanations of social phenomena rather than deferring to paradigmatic allegiances or methodological conventions. The structure-agency relationship serves as the focal point for this analysis not because it exhausts the ontological terrain addressed by dialectical critical realism, but because it provides the most tractable site for demonstrating how explicit ontological commitments translate into methodological requirements. The four-moment dialectic is organized precisely around the temporal and causal relationship between structural conditioning and agential response, making it the clearest available demonstration that different aspects of social reality require different investigative approaches rather than a single paradigmatically determined method.
Through our hybrid model of Bhaskar's four-moment dialectic, we demonstrate how different aspects of dialectically structured social reality require different methodological approaches based on their ontological properties rather than paradigmatic preferences. The four moments are sequential, each presupposing what came before it: 1M represents the norm, the relatively enduring state in which structures persist and condition agency before any disruption. At 1M (non-identity), structures logically predate and condition agency through emergent properties and internal relations, requiring methods that can identify these properties and trace how they contain the capabilities for action, as when an institution's settled rules and resources hold in place, what its members are able to do under normal conditions. At 2E (negativity), this norm gives way to the crisis moment: group formation responds to structural contradictions through social interaction, demanding investigation of how actors recognize contradictions, how collective responses emerge through interaction, and how triggering conditions activate structural mechanisms, as when that same institution's rules and resources prove unable to contain a shock and competing groups mobilise around rival diagnoses of what has gone wrong. At 3L (totality), actors develop reflexive comprehension of structural relationships within partial totalities, necessitating analysis of both necessary connections constituting totalities and actors' developing capacity to envision alternatives to existing arrangements, as those factions settle into a clearer understanding of what the institution has become and what role they now occupy within it. At 4D (transformative praxis), agents create genuinely new social arrangements through conscious intervention constrained by previous dialectical development, requiring methods that distinguish genuine transformation creating new structural possibilities from apparent change that merely reconfigures existing elements, as the institution is rebuilt, for better or worse, around what its most capable actors judged adequate to the crisis.
This paper adopts an unconventional structure appropriate to its theoretical contribution. Rather than demonstrating the framework's utility through empirical case studies, we develop systematic ontological analysis showing how our integrated DCR-Morphogenetic-ICMA framework addresses the structure-agency debate through four dialectical levels. The paper proceeds through detailed examination of each dialectical moment (1M, 2E, 3L, 4D), clarifying the ontological commitments at each level and demonstrating their methodological implications. The goal of this paper is not to prescribe a single correct ontological position. Rather, it demonstrates that taking the epistemic fallacy seriously requires explicit ontological specification, and shows one way of doing this through the integrated DCR-Morphogenetic-ICMA framework. By making ontological assumptions explicit, researchers can assess which methods can adequately access which aspects of social reality under which conditions, rather than deferring to paradigmatic conventions. The absence of empirical demonstration reflects the paper's specific contribution: providing the ontological groundwork that must precede empirical implementation.
The ontological commitments this paper develops are specific to their object. The approach takes structure as analytically prior to agency: structural properties predate the actors who encounter them and constitute the conditioning context within which agency operates, without determining its outcomes. It treats the analytical priority of the intransitive dimension, what exists independently of our knowledge of it, as a prerequisite for methodological choice rather than a consequence of it: ontological questions must be settled, at least provisionally, before epistemological and methodological questions can be meaningfully posed. The material and ideational dimensions of social formations are treated as parallel rather than hierarchically ordered, each requiring investigation on its own terms and neither reducible to the other. And throughout, the approach is committed to stratified ontology: the researcher must always move beneath the empirical surface toward the generative properties and mechanisms that produce what is observable, recognising that the real, the actual, and the empirical are systematically out of phase with one another.
Structural Conditioning at 1M: Non-Identity and Researching Emergent Properties
The first moment (1M) of Bhaskar's dialectic focuses on non-identity as the ground of structures, making this the appropriate starting point for understanding how social reality is constituted. At the 1M level, we research structures because they represent the relatively enduring properties that condition social interaction. These properties are the absent constraints and present capabilities that shape what can happen in any given social situation. Bhaskar introduces his dialectics as follows: 'Dialectical critical realism prioritizes difference over identity, negativity over positivity, absence over presence, totality over its aspects, relations over their relata, structure over agency' (Bhaskar 1993). This prioritization reflects the logical necessity of understanding the conditioning context (structure) before examining how agents respond to and potentially transform that context.
Key Ontological Commitments About Structures

1. Structure Logically Predates Agency
Structures exist before current actions and provide the conditioning context within which agency operates. Margaret Archer argues that “structure necessarily predates the actions which transform it” (Archer 1995, 90). All social action takes place within contexts that are already structured by previous rounds of social interaction. The dialectical object is understanding how structures condition agency at particular moments, recognizing that agency at this level operates within inherited capabilities rather than having immediate transformative capacity.
2. Structures as Emergent Properties with Irreducible Powers
Deepening the specification of structures we can further distinguish a structure with emergent properties characterized by internal and necessary relations that cannot be reduced to the individuals who occupy roles within them. This irreducibility is not a methodological convenience but an ontological claim about the intransitive dimension of social reality. Bhaskar's critical realism insists that structures possess generative mechanisms that operate independently of whether any particular agent recognizes or theorizes them (Bhaskar, 2008). The causal powers of a structure are not the aggregated causal powers of its members; they belong to the relational configuration itself, which persists and operates whether or not the individuals currently occupying roles within it are aware of its operation. Reduction to individual properties therefore does not simplify analysis but misidentifies the object of investigation entirely. Archer defines emergent properties by their "real homogeneity, namely that the relations between its components are internal and necessary ones rather than seemingly regular concatenations of heterogeneous features - of unknown provenance, undetermined internal influence and uncertain duration” (1995, 173). Such properties have "the generative capacity to modify the powers of its constituents in fundamental ways and to exercise causal influences sui generis. This is the litmus test which differentiates between emergence on the one hand and aggregation and combination on the other" (Archer 1995, 174).
For something to count as an emergent property, it must be “invariant in the face of turnover of individuals and relatively resilient to the idiosyncratic preferences and expectations of individuals” (March and Olsen 1984, 741). This means that although specific people with their own ideas can fill certain social roles, their actions are enabled by the capabilities of the properties which can predate and outlast the individuals. For example, even the most powerful dictator is bound by constraints that prevent them from acting unilaterally on every impulse. And while those constraints may not be formally institutionalized, they will be evident to a dictator who wishes to protect their own life, a perceptibility that can exist as a representation/construction of a real property. Assuming the sanity of the dictator, that property will also exist and have powers outside that dictator's mind. Social phenomena must therefore distinguish between emergent properties and simple aggregations of individual behaviours, demonstrating what cannot be explained by individual actions alone.
3. Internal and Necessary Relations Between Components
What distinguishes structures from mere regular patterns is that their components are related through internal and necessary relations rather than external associations. 'Necessary relations' can be specified as relations between specific properties that are contingently necessary: the reasons a relation became established are contingent, a product of how a particular system happened to develop rather than a logical requirement, but once established, the relation is necessary in the sense that its persistence is required for the durability of the system in and through time (Knio 2018). For example, in any modern nation-state, military capacity and state legitimacy constitute internal relations necessary to the existence of effective governance. This governing apparatus cannot be reduced to any of its parts, however, nor could it exist without them. Moreover, despite particular leaders' considerable ability to leverage it, its persistence is independent of any individual, as shown by the continuity of state power across different administrations and leadership transitions.[^1] Military capacity and state legitimacy are thus two key internal and necessary relations within the emergent property of modern state governance particularly in the context of maintaining territorial sovereignty and political order.[^2]
4. Diachronic and Synchronic Emergence
Structures can be distinguished by their temporal constitution. Diachronically emergent properties (those emerging over time) are initially accumulated: they are the contingently necessary properties brought about through a past round of social interactions that exhibit some form of coherence/resilience in the open system of which they are a part (Archer 1995, 183; Knio 2018). On the other hand, synchronic emergence can be described as that which “is constitutively determined by its emergence basis” in time (Sartenaer 2015, 34). From a critical realist perspective, simultaneous constitutive emergence is the moment when the diachronic structure, the collection of individual dispositions,[^3] is confronted by and contingently interacts with its synchronic emergent property of social integration, the potential for indeterminate/creative outcomes or dispositional capabilities. Social integration thus refers to synchronic emergent properties: those that emerge in a particular time as people interact with the properties comprising their current environment that has formed over time (diachronic structure).
5. Agency as Systemic Conditioning at 1M Level
At the 1M level of conditioning, systemic agency refers not to an absence of human agency but to the constraining of actions by necessary and internal relations at the level of properties. This means that structural persistence is perhaps stronger than social constructionism acknowledges, while creative freedom within structures may be greater than strong structuralist positions allow. Through time, the actions of actors embedded in roles expose the dispositional capabilities of structural properties while being embedded within them. Agents are embedded in roles established through past rounds of social interaction, and their actions reveal what structures enable and constrain without fundamentally transforming those structures at the 1M level. The conditioning level thus exhibits relative endurance against internal contradictions and external triggers, as structural properties maintain coherence despite ongoing agential activity. Archer addresses the ontological debate over individuals and groups by stratifying people according to the type of agency they can exercise at key moments along the dialectic, distinguishing agents, people, actors and their roles through what she calls people's emergent properties (PEP).
Overall Methodological Implications of Ontological Commitments at 1M
The investigative logic at 1M is throughout trans-immanent: the researcher asks at every point what must exist at the level of real structures to make what is observable possible (Knio, 2018). This question frames the entire methodological movement at this moment of the dialectic, including the prior determination of periodisation with which the investigation must begin.
Before any synchronic observation becomes meaningful, the researcher must establish a working determination of the temporal boundaries within which the structural formation can be treated as relatively enduring. This is an analytical decision subject to revision: by investigating what is structurally enduring and when it ceases to be so, the researcher generates evidence that may require the initial periodisation to be corrected. Without this prior determination, the researcher cannot distinguish what belongs to the structure from what belongs to a different level in the dialectic movement. It is through the diachronic dimension of the established period, tracing how the structural formation accumulated and consolidated its properties across time, that the object of investigation is first constituted.
The transcendental question is then posed synchronically: at particular moments within the established period, the researcher examines what the material and ideational make observable and infers what must exist beneath that surface at the level of real structures. Following Knio’s parallelism, the material and ideational are treated as two parts of a whole, with the transcendental question directed simultaneously at material and ideational properties. The researcher first identifies what exists within the formation: the elements, positions, arrangements, and ideational contents present within the period under investigation. The materials through which these become accessible vary with the object of investigation; legal and regulatory texts, resource distributions, organisational records, policy documents, historical accounts, and the stated and revealed positions of actors embedded within the material and ideational may all be relevant, but what matters is determined by the structural formation itself rather than a fixed methodological inventory. Having established what exists, the researcher then examines the relations among these elements, which is where the emergent property becomes visible. The analytical task is one of coherence-seeking across these materials, including where they appear contradictory: the researcher must ask what structural property, if real, would explain the observable materials as a coherent whole. Contradictions at the surface are not anomalies but evidence in their own right, since a real structure with internal tensions will produce contradictory empirical manifestations that nonetheless belong to the same formation.
The transcendental question returns repeatedly as the investigation proceeds, and the concept of absence is integral to each of its iterations. What the structural formation forecloses and what contradictions it cannot internally resolve are not gaps in the evidence but positive indicators of causal powers operating at the real level (Bhaskar 1993). As Bhaskar establishes, the real, the actual, and the empirical are out of phase with one another (Bhaskar 1993, 216), which means the researcher must move beneath the level of observation toward the generative properties producing what is observed. Empirical manifestations must never be conflated with the real nature of structural properties, which operate through causal powers and mechanisms that may remain unobservable even as they generate detectable patterns. The investigation at 1M concludes when this repeated movement of transcendental questioning and attention to absence begins to expose tensions within the structural formation that the material and ideational cannot absorb, which is the condition for the transition to 2E.
Realism Negativity in 2E: Principles of Difference
Bhaskar describes this second moment as an edge which creates a difference, a 'spatiotemporal divergence as readily as contradiction, which, if only in the guise of constraint by at least one other, perhaps intrinsic, element, must be seen as essential for change' (Bhaskar 1993, 171). It is at this stage when change, or agency, is introduced that potentially ends the persistence of the structure. At 2E, the necessary relations that bind 1M structures can no longer be sustained, opening structures for social interaction and potential transformation. Structures that were relatively enduring at 1M become open for social interaction at 2E only when triggered and activated, so when their dispositional capabilities, the latent powers and tendencies a structure holds without yet exercising, are exposed to potential reconfiguration through collective response.
Key Ontological Commitments About 2E
1. Triggering and Activation of Structures
'Triggering' is a continuous process as open systems must respond to constant threats to their stability. That is to say that to actually count as systems, emergent properties as systemic dispositions must be persistent and their dispositional capabilities must remain unactivated. An 'activation' challenges the systemic compatibility of an emergent property that then ceases to function as systemically necessary. A 'triggering and activation' refers to the point where the relatively enduring properties of a system are activated, which opens an array of potential dispositional capabilities to be reconfigured. This triggering and activation is a point at which the properties making up the relative endurance of the system, now exist as dispositional capabilities, and entails the exercising of powers to which the system in its current state cannot adequately respond because the necessity of the system no longer functions as it once did.
Separation of the 1M (conditioning level) from the 2E (social interaction) is thus the result of a triggering and activation of structures, which no longer operate as they used to. This activation imbues groups with agential powers allowing them to formulate a response. 2E is the level and phase where a property's dispositional capabilities are expressed and subject to social interaction.
2. Double Morphogenesis of Agency
Archer's double morphogenesis of agency (1995) explains 'Social interaction' is the arena where agents (re)define their relations to structures. After a triggering and activation there is a 'shrinkage of the category of Primary Agents, who become incorporated or transformed into Corporate Agents, thus swelling this category' (Archer 1995, 265). Double morphogenesis is the process whereby corporate agents monitor the actions of other agents to define and redefine their own position in relation to the system's properties, in order to offer a solution (Knio & Sommer, 2024). The foundation of the claims of the corporate agents for a systemic solution will be fallible, however, subject to the ways in which individuals and groups understand it, and to their perceptions of the pre-existent social structures. These perceptions will inevitably relate to their particular interests and the best ways they perceive to fulfil them (Archer 1995; Knio 2020).
3. Social Interaction as Contingent Process
This phase is necessarily contingent: it is open to corporate agents' understanding of the systemic disposition, and their attempts to (re)define it, albeit in a context where the scope of their potential action is constrained by the available dispositional capabilities contained within the interaction, and the ways in which they choose to engage with them. Dispositional capabilities necessitate agency but this will be a form of agency quite different from that of 1M. The outcomes of social interaction are thus subject to a overdetermined contingency. What we can know at the end of 2E are the social agents' conceptions of the necessary relations (as provisional as they might be) and how they seek to fix the problem.
Overall Methodological Implications of Ontological Commitments at 2E
The investigative logic at 2E is no longer primarily directed at identifying what is structurally enduring but at tracking what has become unstable and why. The transition from 1M to 2E is itself a research finding rather than a given: the researcher must establish that the necessary relations binding the structural formation have been exposed to conditions they cannot absorb, generating the spatiotemporal divergence that Bhaskar identifies as the ground of difference and change (Bhaskar 1993, 171). This means the first methodological task at 2E is to identify, within the evidential materials assembled at 1M, the point at which the structural formation begins to exhibit tensions it cannot internally resolve.
The temporal boundaries of 2E are distinct from those of 1M and must be established in their own right. The period under investigation runs from the moment of triggering and activation, when the dispositional capabilities of the structural formation are exposed to conditions that destabilise it, through to the end of social interaction, which is marked by corporate agents having fully formed around their perception of what caused the triggering, what has been done to resolve it, and how their interests are represented in that resolution. This endpoint is not externally imposed but identified through the investigation itself: the researcher must demonstrate that the social interaction has reached a provisional settlement rather than simply selecting an arbitrary cut-off.
With the temporal boundaries established, the investigative focus shifts to corporate agents. Corporate agents are collectivities that form in response to the dispositional capabilities the structural formation makes available, and their formation, regrouping, and eventual consolidation around shared perceptions constitutes the core object of research at this level (Archer 1995). The researcher must identify which corporate agents are active within the interaction, how their formation relates to the structural properties established at 1M, and how their activity reveals those properties as they are contested. Following Knio’s parallelism, this investigation must be conducted across the material and ideational simultaneously, since material interests and ideational positions are both at stake and corporate agents’ activity will be readable in both registers.
Crucially, corporate agents are not simply reacting to the structural contradictions that triggered the interaction. They are continuously monitoring what other agents are doing, and specifically what institutions and organisations other agents are building in response to the situation. Those emergent institutions and organisations are not merely outcomes of the interaction but become objects of monitoring in their own right, shaping how agents regroup and redefine their positions as the interaction unfolds. The researcher must read events within the interaction through the stratified ontology: what is observable at the actual level of events gives clues, when subjected to the transcendental question, about what is present at the real level of structural properties and what corporate agents perceive as absent or needed. The pattern of institutional and organisational construction across the interaction, read in this way, reveals both the dispositional capabilities of the structural formation as agents act upon their understanding of it and the gaps and absences that agents are attempting to fill through their responses.
The central methodological challenge at 2E is that outcomes are overdetermined and contingent: they cannot be read off from structural properties alone, nor attributed to the intentions of any single agent. What is researchable is the contingent process through which competing attempts to resolve structural contradictions interact, and what the researcher can establish at the end of this moment is how corporate agents have come to understand the necessary relations of the formation, what solutions they have sought, and how those attempts have produced outcomes that no single agent fully controlled. The materials relevant to this investigation will vary with the object under investigation, but they will characteristically include evidence of how corporate agents have articulated and repositioned their interests, and what other corporate agencies have merged with them and how they have seen common cause, how they have sought to redefine structural arrangements towards particular ends, and how the institutions and organisations they have built in the course of the interaction have themselves shaped the trajectory of the social interaction toward its provisional resolution.
Totality in 3L
Having moved from the relatively enduring structures of 1M through the social interaction of 2E, in which corporate agents formed around structural contradictions, we now turn to the moment at which those interactions have settled and actors begin to comprehend the totality that has resulted. Returning to Archer, we now know 'what distinguishes an "emergent property" is its real homogeneity, namely that the relations between its components are internal and necessary' (Archer, 1995, p. 173). In this round, we must ask ourselves what relations are truly necessary? What does it mean to be a necessary relation? How do we distinguish 'regular concatenations of heterogeneous features' (the actual) from 'necessary and internal relations' (the real)? And specifically, how do we research them? Doing research at the level of the real is far more onerous, after all, as it entails researching 'mechanisms and causal structures, \[... that\] endure and act quite independently of \[people\]' (Bhaskar 1975, 6). To understand these structures, we need the transcendental question emphasizing necessity, 'what must be there that goes beyond an understanding of the forms (internal relations)?'
It is through Bhaskar's 'totality', the 3L ('Third Level'), that the composition of the true necessary relations can begin to be fathomed: 'the only plausible concept of a totality is that of a partial totality, rife with external as well as internal, and (not the same thing) accidental as well as necessary connections, replete with gaps, discontinuities, voids as well as pockets of thoroughgoing (sub-)totality' (Bhaskar 1993, 252). In other words, 'totality is a structure' (Bhaskar 1975, 368), and the necessary relations that are formative to the whole, are contained within.
Key Ontological Commitments for 3L
1. Partial Totalities with Multiple Connections
Totalities are characterized by their partial nature, containing both necessary and accidental connections, with systematic gaps alongside areas of coherent integration. The most appropriate concept of causality for 3L is holistic causality, understood as “a structured, asymmetrically weighted, differentially charged nexus. Moreover, contrary to Hegel, that the concrete universal is multiple quadruplicity (in which rhythmic processes play an integral part alongside specific mediations), whose moments are in principle separable” (Bhaskar 1993, 252–253). At 3L, actors themselves begin to reflect on which relations are truly necessary versus contingent, a reflection the researcher apprehends through a double hermeneutic reading of actors' accounts, with greater confidence towards the end of 3L.
This does not mean that the empirically measured reflects all necessary and internal relations forming the underlying causality of the system as a whole. It does mean that it is through the dialectic and its systematic approach to defining properties, or understanding how agents come to understand those properties and their interests, that the totality begins to illuminate both the possibilities for re-definition of properties and later the nexus of necessary and internal relations that are contained within it. And we must be reminded that the totality as a structure is an emergent property of some pre-existing properties (1M) and the agentive capacity to redefine or maintain it (2E) (Bhaskar 1993) .
2. Triple Morphogenesis and Actor Formation
According to Archer, the 'reproduction/elaboration' represents the phase in which actors, that are formed as the corporate agency that they represent, begins to take shape, and their roles in the corporate agency as well as their understandings of the social structures that surround them begin to become clear. This clarity, which will be better for some actors than for others, will form what Archer terms the triple morphogenesis of agency (1995). This is the stage in which '\[a\]gency conditions (not determines) who comes to occupy different social roles' and thus who becomes an actor (Archer 1995, 275).[^4]
Actors can thus be seen as emergent properties of corporate agents, as the nature of the situational logics espoused by the collectivities and actors, begins to shape and/or reshape roles for a new round of conditioning. And it is only in understanding the specifics of corporate agents that we can understand the totality of actors. However, as Knio (2018) has suggested, Archer's emphasis on transcendentalism results in her morphogenetic approach undertheorizing the 3L: this is the basis of one of the key arguments for Knio's doctrine of parallelism instead of Archer's analytical dualism. This is why the immanent causality morphogenetic approach, as proposed by Knio, segments the reproduction/elaboration level into two parts, T3-T4 and T4-T5, with T3-T4 representing the 'totality' and T4-T5 representing '4D-agency'.
3. Reflexivity as Inwardized Totality
In T3-T4, actors reflect on the entirety of the relevant properties in what Bhaskar explains as “reflexivity ...\[or\] the inwardized form of totality” (Bhaskar 1993, 379). This process can be researched through Archerian situational logics, and Knio's ‘ideas of adequacy'.[^5] Here actors can absent the absences that comprise the totality. This is because they have become key players in key corporate agencies active in the social interaction and are in a better place to make present the dispositional capabilities uncovered through interaction.
4. Agency as Totality Comprehension at 3L Level
At the 3L level, agency operates through actors' developing comprehension of structural totalities rather than simply responding to structural contradictions (as at 2E). Actors emerge as corporate agencies develop clearer understandings of social structures, representing what Archer calls the formation of actors from corporate agents as structural understanding develops. This represents a qualitative shift from the contingent social interaction of 2E to the reflexive comprehension of totalities that characterizes 3L.
The capacity for reflexivity enables actors to understand not just immediate structural contradictions but the broader totality of relations, or what was always there, including what is absent or excluded.[^6] This comprehension of totality becomes the foundation for the transformative capacity that will emerge at 4D, as actors who understand the full scope of structural relations are better positioned to envision and create alternative arrangements.
Overall Methodological Implications of Ontological Commitments at 3L
The transition from 2E to 3L is marked by the provisional settlement of social interaction and the emergence of actors who begin to reflect on what, from their perspective, was always already there as a structural form. The researcher must establish this transition through the concept of ideas as self-explication: actors articulate, through the ideational resources available to them, what the structural formation consists of, what produced it, what it requires, and what within it corresponds to their interests and positions as shaped through the corporate agency of 2E. This self-explication serves simultaneously as a theoretical object and as a methodological check on the completion of the 2E interaction, since actors who have not yet reached this reflective articulation remain within the 2E moment. The researcher must therefore identify, within the evidential materials, the point at which actors’ accounts shift from reactive positioning within an ongoing interaction to reflective comprehension of a formation they now treat as given.
The temporal boundaries of 3L run from this moment of emergent reflection through to the point at which actors have achieved what Knio identifies as ideas as adequacy. The researcher identifies this endpoint through the evidential materials rather than imposing it externally.
Situational logics provide the institutionally situated rationality through which actors within particular structural positions orient to the totality (Archer 1995), giving shape to what matters within the self-explication and constraining the range of reflexive responses available to actors in different positions. The triple morphogenesis of agency provides the theoretical pathway through which this reflexive behaviour is understood because it concerns the internal deliberative processes through which actors evaluate their structural situation from within the positions and orientations that corporate agency has produced.
The investigative challenge at 3L is that this reflexive process happens substantially within actors rather than being directly observable in institutional construction or corporate positioning. The double hermeneutic is therefore particularly relevant here as a methodological note: the researcher is working with actors’ own accounts of a formation they are simultaneously inhabiting and reflecting upon, and must maintain analytical distance sufficient to assess those accounts against the material and ideational properties established at the prior moments, while recognising that actors’ self-explication is itself evidence of those properties as they are inwardised. As at 1M and 2E, the transcendental question and the concept of absence remain operative: what actors identify as absent or needed within the totality, and what the material and ideational dimensions of the formation make available or foreclose for reflexive elaboration, give the researcher access to the real level of properties through actors’ reflective accounts.
This is facilitated by actors paying attention to the new patterns, or rhythmics that have formed from the old, and 'absenting the necessity' of those rhythmics, i.e., acting in recognition that a change has occurred. As researchers, we can reflect on this process of absenting through the double hermeneutic moment which begins to build an understanding of the totality being conceived by those actor(s). This double hermeneutic is possible because, while actors were embedded participants in the process as it unfolded, the researcher can reflect on the process in its entirety after the fact: an actor's account may have been right enough for the position from which they encountered events, but the researcher, working with the benefit of hindsight, may see more of the totality than was visible to any single actor in the midst of it. This will take into consideration the actors' reflections on (a) their interests, (b) the unfolding of events with the passage of time, and (c) the infrastructure and organizations that have developed through this social interaction, and finally, (d) the ways in which those actors come to understand and use the new facilities, tools and organizations. As actors' reflections move toward ideas as adequacy, the researcher's apprehension of which relations are necessary becomes correspondingly more confident.
Ideas as adequacy marks the completion of this process: as the material and ideational dimensions hang together as a coherent whole from the actors’ perspective, constituting a settled understanding of the totality and their position within it. The researcher identifies this not as a declared endpoint but when actors’ accounts no longer exhibit the tensions and gaps characteristic of the 2E moment and begin to constitute a stable basis for the forward-looking elaboration of agency that carries into 4D.
Agency in 4D Realism: Transformation
4D (‘Agency’) is the final phase in the four part dialectic, described by Bhaskar as the ‘re-totalization of the periphery in the mutual recognitions of identities-in-difference and unity-in-diversity, mediated therefore by mutual recognition of differential (personal, social, local, etc.) identities and involving a degree of recentrification (psychic, social, local and global) in a transformed transformative praxis for the re-totalization of the human race’ (Bhaskar 1993, 11). This is a more modest claim than it first appears: as Bhaskar explains, ‘4D presupposes 3L presupposes 2E presupposes 1M’ (Bhaskar 1993, 8). Transformation logically requires totality, which requires difference, which requires negativity, which requires non-identity. The capacity for recentrification is thus constrained by the dialectical process that precedes it, and specifically by the necessary relations established in the totality arrived at in 3L. 4D represents the capacity for conscious human agency to transform existing structures by filling in absences and resolving contradictions, creating genuinely new social arrangements.
Key Ontological Commitments About 4D
1. Constrained Transformation
4D is the ground of intentional causality, but taking the dialectic seriously requires seeing it as constrained by Bhaskar’s statement that “agency \[4D\] is sustained philosophically by an emergent powers materialist orientation and substantively by the concept of four planar social being in nature with the moral evolution of the species, like the future generally, open. Its dialectics are at the site of ideological and material struggles, but also of absolute reason” (Bhaskar 1993, 369). 4D is thus grounded in the materialist totality of 3L and the ‘four planar social being’ of actors who emerge alongside this totality, defined by Bhaskar as ‘the planes (a) material transactions with nature, (b) inter-/intra-subjective relations, \[…\] (c) social relations sui generis, defining the level of social institutions, and (d) the stratification of the personality’ (Bhaskar 1993, 374). 4D can therefore be seen as the capacity of actors to understand the emergent totality and, through their positions within it, to ‘absent the absences’ by creating new social forms that recognise this conglomeration. Transformative capacity builds through the entire dialectical sequence rather than emerging spontaneously: actors’ comprehension of totality at 3L enables specific forms of transformation while constraining others through the presuppositional chain in which 4D presupposes 3L presupposes 2E presupposes 1M.
2. Objectification of New Social Forms
The building of new social forms, or ‘objectification’, depends upon actors’ understanding of the entirety of the ‘absented’ or potential new totality, that is, the new 1M, and their capacities through their social roles to build those social forms. 4D, following from 3L, is also the point at which the ethics of Bhaskar and of Spinoza, as discussed in Knio (2018), become clear. This ethical framework diverges from conventional morality by defining ethics not as adherence to principles or rules but as the actualisation of power: ethics becomes the capacity to act effectively on a comprehensive understanding of totality, and agency in this sense is the ability to comprehend the totality and to act upon it. As Smith explains, “ethics defines a person not by what they are in principle (their essence), but by what they can do, what they are capable of (their power)” (2001, 178).
3. Spatial Dimensions of Transformative Power
While an awareness of space is necessary at 3L to account for the capacities of properties to merge within different territories, at 4D space provides the contextual landscape that shapes the potentialities of actors to cast new social forms: the power of actors is relative to their potential to exert influence over a particular space. At 4D, properties have come together in a specific way through the dialectical process to result in a specific form, and that form will be necessary for the existence of the properties for the next 1M conditioning; with each level of emergence, we can assess the degree to which properties exist alongside each other in states of coherence or resilience against the background of an open system (Knio 2020, 469). The spatial dimension is crucial because transformation requires not just understanding totalities but having the spatial reach to institutionalise new social forms across relevant scales and territories.
4. Agency as Transformative Praxis at 4D Level
At the 4D level, agency operates as transformative praxis: the capacity to create genuinely new social arrangements through conscious intervention based on comprehensive understanding of totalities. This represents the culmination of the dialectical development of agency from systemic conditioning (1M) through responsive interaction (2E) and reflexive comprehension (3L) to transformative action (4D). Actors at this level possess what Bhaskar calls the capacity for ‘transformed transformative praxis’: they can not only understand existing totalities but actively reshape them through conscious intervention, an active ‘absenting the absences’ that fills in structural gaps and resolves contradictions identified through reflexive comprehension at 3L. This transformative capacity is constrained by the understanding developed at previous dialectical levels, making 4D the culmination rather than the negation of earlier moments.
Overall Methodological Implications of Ontological Commitments at 4D
At 4D the dialectical movement reaches its culmination, and the investigative logic shifts from establishing what structural properties exist and how actors have come to understand them toward examining what actor(s) are in a position to exercise their understanding of ideas as adequacy. The central research question concerns how adequacy translates into structural restructuring and new institutional formation: what actors understood as adequate, how they read that adequacy, and what changes they effected on the basis of it. The researcher reads the material and ideational dimensions of this translation simultaneously, attending to how actors’ adequate comprehension of the totality is expressed in the institutional forms they reconstitute and create.
A prior and non-trivial methodological task is establishing which actors are structurally positioned to act on their adequacy. This cannot be assumed in advance: the structural positions that carry genuine capacity to effect institutional change are themselves research findings, and defaulting to formally prominent positions such as heads of government or executive leadership may obscure where structural leverage actually resides within a given formation. The researcher must trace, from the evidential base assembled across the prior moments, which positions within the restructured formation carry the capacity to translate adequate comprehension into institutional reality, and whose adequacy therefore has the greatest bearing on what is institutionalised.
Temporal constraints introduce a further analytical dimension. Actors have finite time and attention, and institutional change is intrinsically difficult: adequate comprehension of the totality does not translate automatically or completely into institutions. The gap between what actors understand as adequate and what they are able to institutionalise within the practical constraints of their position is therefore itself analytically significant. Where actors concentrate their restructuring efforts, what they judge most essential to act upon within those constraints, and what they leave unaddressed gives the researcher a further indication of how adequacy is being conceived and prioritised. The resulting institutional landscape is always a partial and imperfect realisation of adequacy, and that partiality is informative rather than merely residual.
The spatial dimension of 4D is a derivative rather than independent analytical concern. The spatial distribution of structural prominence is a product of how adequacy has been institutionalised: where certain structures are reconstituted as more prominent through the acting out of adequacy, they come to exert an outsized influence on the spatial organisation of the formation. A structure that emerges as prominently institutionalised from the 4D moment will extend its logic across the spatial domains in which it operates, potentially elevating seemingly peripheral locations into structural significance. The spatial container of a given region, its importance relative to others, is therefore not given by geography but produced by the structural weight of what adequacy has made prominent within it.
Conclusion
This paper has demonstrated that a productive way to transcend the qualitative-quantitative divide in social science research can be rooted in making specific ontological foundations clear. Following Bhaskar's intransitive/transitive distinction, we have established our two-part argument: first, the necessity for explicit social ontology to avoid the epistemic fallacy; second, a systematic example through integrating Bhaskar's dialectical framework with Archer's morphogenetic approach.
Each dialectical level provides concrete ontological commitments that directly inform methodological choices: structures as emergent properties conditioning agency (1M), group formation responding to structural contradictions (2E), actors developing reflexive comprehension of totalities (3L), and transformative praxis and the capabilities of creating new arrangements (4D). These commitments transcend methodological divisions by suggesting that different aspects of dialectically structured reality require different approaches based on what exists.
However, our choice of critical realism as the ontological foundation reflects our own theoretical commitments rather than methodological necessity. The central argument of this paper extends beyond critical realism itself: any ontological position must undergo this same exercise of making explicit assumptions about the intransitive dimension to avoid the epistemic fallacy. This requirement is not ontological pluralism, the claim that researchers may legitimately choose among competing ontologies according to which methods they prefer, since that would simply invert the epistemic fallacy rather than avoid it. The claim instead is that whichever ontological commitments a researcher holds, whether atomistic individualism where only individuals possess causal powers, structural functionalism where social systems are organic wholes with functional requirements, or social constructionism where reality is constituted through intersubjective meaning-making, those commitments carry methodological implications that should be made explicit and followed rather than left to operate invisibly. The pluralism this paper defends is epistemological rather than ontological: a single stratified social reality, of the kind dialectical critical realism specifies, requires multiple methods to access its different levels and aspects, not because reality is plural, but because no single method can adequately capture every stratum of a reality that is irreducibly differentiated.
This raises the crucial question of ontological adequacy. There can and should be disagreement about our specific ontological choices: whether structures possess the emergent properties we claim, whether social reality consists of atomistic individuals or emergent collectivities, or whether meaning-making processes constitute or merely interpret an independent reality. These disagreements are resolved through what Bhaskar calls "judgmental rationality", the capacity to assess which ontological specifications offer better explanations of the phenomena under investigation (Bhaskar 1975) . This creates possibilities for both comparing between different ontological positions and debating specific claims within positions, moving methodological debates toward more productive ground. The implications extend beyond individual research to methodological innovation in social science education and interdisciplinary organization. Instead of training researchers in competing paradigms defined by epistemological commitments, we can begin with ontological questions about social reality, demonstrating how these commitments inform methodological choices. This promises principled foundations for methodological decision-making while enabling genuine interdisciplinary dialogue around explicitly articulated ontological foundations.
This represents a fundamental reorientation from paradigmatic allegiances toward methodological approaches grounded in systematic ontological analysis. The result is not the elimination of methodological diversity, but its reorganization around transparent ontological foundations that enable both theoretical development and empirical investigation to proceed on more secure ground.
